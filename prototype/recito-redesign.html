The Voice in the Margin

For most of history, to read was to be heard. Listening to a page is an old habit we are picking up again.

By Ines Okafor · 4 minute read

In the late fourth century, a young teacher of rhetoric visited the bishop of Milan and found him doing something odd. The bishop sat with a book open in front of him, and his eyes moved across the page, but his voice was silent and his tongue was still. The visitor thought it strange enough to write down, and he spent a paragraph guessing at the reason. Perhaps the bishop was saving his voice. Perhaps he wanted to avoid questions from the people who drifted in and out of the room.

We read that passage now and smile, because silent reading is the only kind most of us practise. But the visitor’s surprise tells us something about his world. A text, for him, was a script for the voice. Reading meant performing it, usually for other people, and a book that nobody spoke was a book at rest.

The pages themselves were built for speech. Early manuscripts ran their words together without spaces, so the surest way to find where one word ended was to sound the line out. Punctuation, when it arrived, marked where a reader should breathe. Libraries were noisy rooms, and a scholar who wanted quiet had to ask for it.

What the eye gave up

Silent reading spread slowly, helped along by spaces between words and by the private study. It brought real gains. A silent reader can skim, double back, and hold two passages against each other. She can read faster than anyone can speak, and she can read things she would never say out loud.

A book that nobody spoke was a book at rest.

Something was traded away, though. A voice sets a pace, and a pace is a kind of attention. When a sentence is spoken you cannot slide past it the way the eye slides past a familiar paragraph. Rhythm does part of the remembering for you, which is why people who cannot recall a page of prose can still recite a poem they learned at nine.

Teachers have long known this. Proofreaders read copy aloud because the ear catches the doubled word that the eye forgives. Writers do the same with their drafts, and for the same reason: a sentence that trips the tongue will usually trip the reader as well.

Reading with both

The interesting case is the one in between, where the eye and the ear work on the same text at once. Studies of reading while listening tend to find modest gains in comprehension for long or difficult material, and larger ones for people reading in a second language. The voice carries the reader through the hard stretch, and the page stays there for the moment she wants to stop and look again.

That pairing used to need a second person. A parent read while a child followed the line with a finger. Now a synthetic voice can do it, and the finger is a band of colour that moves along the sentence. The technology is new, but the arrangement is very old, and it works for the old reasons.

None of this is an argument against silence. It is a reminder that the quiet page was an invention, and a recent one. When you let an essay be read to you while your eyes rest on the words, you are doing what the visitor in Milan would have recognised at once as reading.
